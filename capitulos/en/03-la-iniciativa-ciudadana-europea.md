# Chapter 3: The European Citizens' Initiative

We had just discovered the master key to the "Exceptional Reserve" together with Athena, but we immediately came face to face with an incontestable reality: a brilliant idea in the head of a programmer in Alcorcón does not move the gears of international diplomacy a single millimeter.

Why would the bureaucrats of Geneva or the government of Athens listen to a random citizen who sent them emails translated into Greek? We needed an instrument with indisputable legal weight, something that would force institutions to sit at the table without being able to send us away with a courtesy letter.

And that instrument exists. It's called **European Citizens' Initiative (ECI)**.

## Beyond the click: Real direct democracy

Signatures on the Internet usually end up in the recycling bin because they do not commit anyone; They are testimonial. Instead, the ECI is a direct democracy tool introduced by the Lisbon Treaty. If you manage to articulate a proposal within the powers of the European Union and gather the citizen support required by law, the European Commission and the European Parliament are **obliged by regulation** to formally receive you, open a legislative debate and issue a reasoned official response.

It was the lever we needed. But the European regulation contained a first trap to prevent anyone from collapsing the institutions: before being able to collect a single signature, you had to officially register the initiative. And to register it, the law imposed a requirement that made my blood run cold: the **Committee of Organizers**.

It was necessary to bring together a minimum of **seven citizens residing in seven different Member States** of the European Union willing to commit their name, surname and personal data as official promoters.

For someone like me—an introvert by nature, who enjoys spending idle hours in front of the screen thinking about his projects and with hardly any contacts outside of my daily environment—convincing six strangers in six European countries seemed like an absolutely impossible mission.

But the network works miracles when the cause is just and shared. Through professional contacts, civic technology enthusiasts and complicities that we were weaving step by step, we got citizens from seven countries to join the promoting committee. We had the seven keys needed to open the door to Brussels.

## The strong idea: The digital Denomination of Origin

With the team in place, it was time to draft the legal heart of the proposal. We had to explain to the European Union why Antikythera and why the `.ia` domain. And that is where the analogy that everyone understands the first time arose: the **Denomination of Origin**.

When you go to a supermarket and buy a bottle of olive oil, wine or cheese with the Protected Designation of Origin seal, you don't need to carry a chemical laboratory in your pocket. The seal guarantees three fundamental things: **demonstrated intrinsic quality, absolute traceability from the origin and a legal person who is responsible before the law if it deceives you**.

Now look at what is happening on the Internet today with Artificial Intelligence. We live in the wild west: any company or individual can pay fifty dollars to an office on the small Caribbean island of Anguilla, register a **`.ai`** domain, and deploy opaque models that collect data without control or audit. The most transformative technology in human history operates under a tropical flag of convenience.

Why wouldn't Europe create the world's first digital Denomination of Origin?

In most European languages ​​with Latin roots—Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese or Romanian—we do not say *AI*; We say **Artificial Intelligence (`AI`)**. And the `.ia` domain was completely free at the root of the Internet, waiting for a noble purpose.

If we associated the `.ia` code with the island of Antikythera through the Exceptional Reserve, we were not creating a private business: we were creating a **European sovereign asset**. A public infrastructure where only those artificial intelligences that comply with the highest ethical, privacy and transparency standards of the European Union could be housed. Anyone browsing under a `.ia` domain would immediately know that they were looking at a reliable, auditable technology with human guarantees.

With this structured vision, Athena and I drafted the two main objectives of the initiative and sent the formal file to the European Commission.

The countdown began. We were about to find out if the jurists in Brussels would crush our dream under a mountain of technicalities or if, for the first time in history, Europe would dare to challenge the map of the Internet.

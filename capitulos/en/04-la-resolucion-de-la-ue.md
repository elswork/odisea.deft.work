# Chapter 4: October 6: The European Union resolution

Between the sending of the forms to Brussels and the official resolution, weeks of tense calm passed that seemed eternal to me.

The European Commission subjects each Citizen Initiative to relentless legal scrutiny before accepting it for processing. It is the filter where the vast majority of citizen proposals shipwreck: the lawyers of the European Union scrutinize every comma to check if the object of the request falls "manifestly outside the framework of the Commission's powers" or if it lacks fit into the community treaties.

I was fully aware of who we were playing against. For more than two years, from ICANN to international experts, I had been told that aspiring to the `.ia` ccTLD without being a UN State was a technical and legal chimera. If the legal services in Brussels found the slightest crack, they would sign a resolution of rejection and this whole trip would have ended with a diplomatic door slamming.

## The Strasbourg verdict

It was an ordinary afternoon in front of the screen at my house in Alcorcón, with the coffee already cold and the inbox open almost by inertia, without getting my hopes up too much. Suddenly, a message with an institutional sender from the General Secretariat of the European Commission broke the routine. There were no congratulations or fireworks in the matter, only the bureaucratic sobriety typical of community institutions: the notification of a formal act adopted by the College of Commissioners.

I opened the attachment with my heart in my mouth.

Meeting in Strasbourg on **6 October 2026**, the College of Commissioners of the European Union had formally adopted **Implementing Decision C(2026) 7098 final**. Not only did they accept our proposal for processing; They officially declared the initiative registered under the legal code **`ECI(2026)000012`**:

> **«Creation of the European Digital District (.IA) for the Governance of Sovereign Artificial Intelligence»**.

When I read the basics of law with Athena, I got goosebumps. The Commission's jurists explicitly recognized that the European Union **has full legal powers** to legislate on sovereign internet infrastructure and the `.ia` domain, based on articles 16, 114, 172 and 173 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU).

That resolution was an amendment to all of those who for years had told us that "it couldn't be done." We were no longer dreamers on the Internet arguing with Web3 speculators or collecting informal signatures. We had in hand a binding legal act of the European Union that certified that the creation of the Denomination of Origin '.ia' in Antikythera was legitimate, viable and within Community competence.

## From relief to vertigo: The million signatures

The initial euphoria lasted exactly as long as it took me to process the second part of the European Union regulation.

Having passed the legal filter of Brussels did not mean having won the war; It just meant that we were given permission to jump onto the battlefield. The European Commission does not draft a community law simply because your idea is brilliant. The Treaty of Lisbon requires a democratic litmus test: collecting **one million verified signatures from citizens of at least seven countries of the Union** within the non-extendable period of 365 days from the opening of the central voting system.

One million signatures. No retweets, no anonymous support on social networks, but citizens of the flesh proving their identity with their ID or passport in the official electronic ballot box of the European Commission.

Fortunately, the European regulation does not throw you into the ring overnight. The law granted us a margin of up to six months from official registration to prepare for continental deployment before activating collection. I decided to speed up that deadline as much as possible to arrive with the best possible tools: **April 6, 2027** would be D-Day, the exact moment in which the community electronic ballot box would open its doors and the decisive year to gather the million wills would begin to run.

To try to spread the collection of signatures as much as possible, I created a meeting page to recruit supporters: [anticitera.deft.work/6abril](https://anticitera.deft.work/es/6abril/).

And it is precisely at that point that we find ourselves today: with the support of the community law in hand, an entire continent to mobilize and the certainty that the ball is no longer in the court of the Brussels bureaucrats, but in the hands of citizens: citizens like you, with the right to sign and with an environment of friends and family that can also participate.
